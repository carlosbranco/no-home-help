The project

No Home Help is a free, open-source directory for people who need food, a safe place to sleep, health care, hygiene facilities, documents, benefits, legal support or help finding work.

The aim is simple: someone using an old phone, a slow connection or public Wi-Fi should be able to find the first useful local contact in less than 30 seconds. The directory is not a service provider and cannot guarantee a bed, meal, appointment, payment or job.

The first directory release created contribution pages for cities across Europe and the Americas. Detailed content is now being researched city by city, starting with large urban areas where one verified page can help the greatest number of people.

How information is published

  • Local language: city pages are written in a language commonly used locally.
  • Practical first: emergency contacts, shelter access and food appear before background information.
  • Source-backed: addresses, telephone numbers, access rules and opening times must have a current official or provider source.
  • Honest uncertainty: pages explain when someone must call, register, receive a referral or wait for a vacancy.
  • Dated review: detailed listings show when the information was last checked.
  • Open contribution: anyone can propose a correction or add a verified local service through GitHub.
  • Lightweight and private: the site uses no JavaScript, cookies, tracking, images or external fonts.

Draft city pages are clearly marked and kept out of search-engine indexes until useful local information has been checked.

Which cities come first

The next research phase starts with the world's most populous cities. The United Nations World Urbanization Prospects 2025 identifies Jakarta, Dhaka, Tokyo, New Delhi and Shanghai as the five largest cities under its harmonized city definition.

Population is a research priority, not a homelessness estimate. A larger population does not automatically mean a higher homelessness count or rate. Housing costs, income, conflict, migration, public policy, available accommodation and the way a city counts homelessness all affect the reported number.

Homelessness statistics in major cities

This is not a world ranking. There is no current, standardized global city dataset that makes these figures directly comparable. The table presents selected official counts and keeps each source's geography, date and definition visible.

Toronto 12,196 22 October 2025 Point-in-time estimate: 9,026 in the city shelter and family support system, 1,446 outdoors and 1,724 in provincially administered settings. City of Toronto
Brussels-Capital Region 9,777 November 2024 Regional count using a broad homelessness framework. Bruss'help reported that nearly 1,000 of the people counted were living on the street. Bruss'help

Why the figures differ

  • One night or a full year: London's figure counts different people seen over twelve months; most other rows are snapshots.
  • Sheltered or unsheltered: some counts include shelters and temporary accommodation, while Paris and Tokyo use much narrower coverage.
  • City or region: Brussels is a capital region and Tokyo's figure covers its 23 special wards. Administrative boundaries are not equivalent.
  • Hidden homelessness: people staying temporarily with friends, in unsafe housing or outside the service system are often missed.
  • Different years: official city censuses are not conducted on a shared international schedule.

For these reasons, the raw figures must not be used to claim that one city has a higher homelessness rate than another. They are included to show the scale reported by local authorities and to make gaps in global data visible.
